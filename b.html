Critical Circuit: Transimpedance Amplifier

This is the heart of the STM. It converts picoamp tunneling current into millivolt signals your ADC can read. Build this first and test it before anything else.

Keep Rf lead length < 5mm. Stray capacitance kills bandwidth. 2. Guard ring around inverting input pin. Drive guard at V_out potential. 3. Shield the tip wire. Coax or twisted pair, short as possible. 4. Decouple V+ and V- pins with 100nF ceramic RIGHT AT the pins. 5. Analog ground separate from digital ground. Star-point connection. 6. NO switching power supplies near this circuit. Linear regulators only. 7. Deadbug construction on copper-clad board is BETTER than a PCB for this. Build Sequence
Step 01
Day 1-2

Etch Your First Tips

Cut 10-15mm lengths of 0.25mm tungsten wire. Mix 2M NaOH solution (80g per liter distilled water). Suspend wire vertically through stainless steel ring cathode submerged in solution. Apply 4-7V DC. Wait ~20 minutes. The wire necks and drops. The remaining stub is your tip.

Etch 10 tips minimum. Expect 6-7 good ones. Inspect under magnification — you want a smooth taper to a fine point. Store tips in a clean container.

NaOH is caustic. Gloves and glasses MANDATORY. Work near a water source. If it gets on skin, flush immediately with water for 15 minutes. This is not optional safety theater — concentrated NaOH causes chemical burns.
Pro tip from Dan Berard: 7.5V, 4M NaOH, 26°C, 2mm immersion length gives 63.5% yield for tips under 10nm radius.
Step 02
Day 2-4

Build the Transimpedance Amplifier

This is the hardest electronics subsystem. Build it first, test it alone, prove it works before touching anything else. Deadbug construction on copper-clad board. OPA124 + 100MΩ feedback resistor + 1.5pF cap. Power with ±15V linear supply or 3S LiPo.

Test: apply 1V through a 1GΩ resistor to the inverting input. That's 1nA. You should read ~100mV at the output. Check noise floor on your scope — you want <10mV peak-to-peak.

If you don't have a 1GΩ resistor for testing, two 500MΩ in series works. Or apply 0.1V through 100MΩ for the same 1nA test current.
Step 03
Day 3-5

Build the DAC/ADC Interface

Wire the DAC8814 and LTC2326-16 to the Teensy 4.1 via SPI. Write basic firmware: output a voltage on one DAC channel, read it back on the ADC. Verify linearity. Add the scan waveform generator — a simple raster pattern (sawtooth X, stepped Y) output on DAC channels.

Build the piezo driver: op-amp gain stage that takes 0-3.3V DAC output and amplifies to the range your piezo needs (±50 to ±200V depending on scanner). Start with low voltage for testing.

Step 04
Day 4-7

Machine the STM Body

CNC mill the main body from 6061 aluminum. The design is simple: a rigid block that holds the piezo scanner (with tip) above a sample stage, with the coarse approach screw coming in from the side or below. Key requirements: mechanical rigidity, thermal mass, and a clear path from tip to sample with minimal overhang.

Refer to Dan Berard's design or MechRedPanda's 3D-printed version for geometry. Your CNC advantage: tighter tolerances = better stability = longer scan times.

The STM body should be as thermally massive as practical. Aluminum's high thermal conductivity means temperature changes propagate uniformly rather than creating differential expansion. A solid block with pockets milled out beats a skeletal frame.
Step 05
Day 5-7

Prepare the Piezo Scanner

Take a 27mm piezo buzzer disc. Score the top electrode into 4 equal quadrants using a razor blade and straightedge. Each quadrant becomes one axis control signal. Apply voltage to opposite quadrants for X or Y deflection. Apply to all four for Z (dome mode).

Mount the tip to the center of the disc using conductive epoxy or a tiny drop of solder. The tip should protrude 1-2mm below the disc plane.

Test: apply voltage to each quadrant pair and verify deflection using your USB microscope or a laser pointer reflection.

Step 06
Day 6-8

Build the Coarse Approach

Mount the 28BYJ-48 stepper to drive the M3×0.5 fine-pitch screw. The screw advances the STM head (or sample stage) toward the tip. Each half-step = 0.5mm / 2048 ≈ 0.24µm. The piezo Z range is ~500nm, so each step must be less than that. 0.24µm = 240nm. Safe.

The approach algorithm: extend piezo Z fully, take one motor step, retract piezo fully, repeat. When tunneling current is detected during retract, stop motor. Z-feedback takes over.

TURN THE MOTOR OFF after approach is complete. Motor coil heat causes thermal drift that will wreck your scan. Mechanical friction or a set screw holds position after power-off.
Step 07
Day 7-9

Build the Vibration Isolation Stack

Layer 1: Heavy granite plate or steel plate on Viton O-rings or sorbothane pads, sitting on your workbench (away from CNC and lathe). Layer 2: STM assembly sits on the plate. Optional Layer 3: suspend the entire assembly from bungee cords attached to a frame or ceiling hook.

Scan at night when traffic, HVAC, and household activity are minimal. Your house's mechanical noise floor will be your limiting factor in Phase 0.

Step 08
Day 8-10

Write the Scan Software

Teensy firmware: Z-feedback PID loop (read tunneling current, adjust Z voltage to maintain constant current setpoint). Raster scan (step X, read Z, step X, read Z... increment Y, repeat). Stream data to PC over USB serial.

PC software: receive XY + Z data, render as grayscale image. Python + matplotlib is fine for first light. The image is a height map — bright = tip retracted (high feature), dark = tip extended (low feature).

Start with a very slow scan speed (minutes per image). Speed is not the goal — stability is. Once you have a stable image, you can optimize scan rate.
Step 09
Day 9-12

First Light

Cleave your HOPG with scotch tape. Mount it on the sample stage. Install your best-looking etched tip. Set bias voltage to ~100mV. Set tunneling current setpoint to ~1nA. Power up the TIA. Start the coarse approach.

When tunneling current appears, the feedback loop takes over. Start a slow scan. Your first image will probably look like garbage — noise, drift, tip artifacts. That's normal. Iterate: try different tips, adjust PID gains, improve vibration isolation, scan slower.

When you see the hexagonal carbon lattice — 0.246nm spacing, six-fold symmetry — you have resolved individual atoms. In your bedroom. For less than $300.

HOPG is forgiving. If your first scan shows smooth terraces with atomic steps (0.335nm height), you're close. If you see periodic structure at the right spacing, you're there. Compare against published HOPG STM images to confirm.

Standing on Shoulders

Dan Berard — dberard.com

The gold standard DIY STM. Atomic resolution on HOPG for ~$100. Teensy-based. Extensively documented with schematics, firmware, and build photos. Your primary reference for electronics and firmware design.

OpenSTM — github.com/Dimsmary/OpenSTM

Peer-reviewed open-source STM. ESP32-based. ~$300. Published in HardwareX journal. Battery-powered analog section. Good reference for clean power supply design.

MechRedPanda — github.com/MechRedPanda/red-panda-stm

3D-printed STM with atomic resolution (2024). Teensy 4.1. PA-CF printed structure. Featured on Hackaday. Proves that even plastic structures can achieve atomic imaging with proper design.

John Alexander — john-alexander42.github.io

The original sub-$100 STM design. Pioneered the unimorph disc scanner approach that all subsequent DIY STMs use. Historical reference — study the design philosophy.
